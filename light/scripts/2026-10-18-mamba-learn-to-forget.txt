# What If AI Could Learn to Forget? (Mamba Explained) · 8:05 · 18 Oct 2026 (from the YouTube transcript, caption typos fixed). Most views so far (451).
Every AI chatbot you've ever used has a secret weakness. The longer you talk to it, the harder it has to work. Not a little harder, much harder. Double the text and the core of the model doesn't do twice the work. It does about four times the work. Make it 10 times longer, around 100 times the work. That's why long documents are so heavy to process and why there's a limit on how much you can paste in. Then two researchers asked a strange question. What if an AI could learn to forget? Stick with me because by the end, forgetting is going to sound like a superpower.
First, the machine they were trying to beat. Almost every big chatbot today is built on something called a transformer. And at its heart is a trick called attention. When the model reads a word, it looks back at every word that came before it and asks, how relevant are you to me right now? Every word checks every other word. That's why transformers are so good. Nothing gets lost. A name on page one can still connect to a sentence on page 50. But it's also the problem. Imagine a meeting where every time someone speaks, you have to reread the whole transcript from the very beginning. In a 5-minute meeting, that's fine. In a 5-hour meeting, it's a nightmare. That's attention. It never summarizes. It keeps everything and it rereads everything.
There's another family of models that works the opposite way. They're called recurrent models. They read one word at a time and carry a small memory forward, like reading a novel and keeping the story in your head instead of flipping back to page one. These models are fast. 10 times more text, about 10 times more work, not 100, but their memory is small, and they had no good way to decide what deserved to go into it. Then a newer branch of this family showed up from a very unexpected place. Not a chatbot lab, rocket science. They're called state space models, and their roots go back to 1960 to the math used to track rockets and spacecraft. They were great at smooth signals like audio and they could handle extremely long sequences. But on language they kept losing to transformers.
Albert Gu at Carnegie Mellon and Tri Dao at Princeton figured out why. These models treated every input exactly the same way. An important name, a filler word, a typo, same treatment. Imagine someone tells you a phone number, but between every digit they say um, uh, like, you know. You instantly ignore the noise and keep the digits. These models couldn't. They could keep track of time but not of meaning. So in late 2023, Gu and Dao released a new model. They called it Mamba. Now here's the twist. Mamba looks at each word as it arrives and decides what to do with it, right in that moment. They call this selection. Should this word go into memory or pass straight through? Should I hold on to what I know or wipe it clean and start fresh? An important word gets written in firmly. A meaningless one gets ignored. And if the topic suddenly changes, Mamba can reset its memory, the way you clear your head when someone changes the subject. To be clear, Mamba's memory doesn't grow as the text grows. It stays the same small size. That's exactly why it has to choose. Think of a notebook with only one page. You can't write down everything. So, you write down only what matters and you erase what doesn't.
It sounds like a small change. But it broke something. The old models were fast precisely because they treated every word the same. That let them use a math shortcut and process a whole sequence in one big sweep. Once every word gets its own treatment, that shortcut is gone. So, Mamba was smarter, but in danger of becoming slow. This is where the hardware comes in. The graphics chip that runs AI has two kinds of memory. A big one that's slow to reach and a tiny one right next to the processor that's extremely fast. Often, the slow part isn't the math. It's moving the data back and forth. Tri Dao was already famous for this. His earlier technique called flash attention made transformers much faster by moving less data. He did the same for Mamba. Load only the small ingredients into the fast memory. Do all the work there and write back only the final answer. During training, don't store the huge in between results. Just recalculate them when they're needed. According to the paper, that made this step 20 to 40 times faster than a standard version. Smarter and fast at the same time.
Then they put it to the test. Quick question for you. If I hid a few numbers in a wall of random noise, could you pick them out? Of course you could. The older state space model in their test got about 18% of that task right. Mamba got over 99%. Then came the hard part, memory. The model sees a pair like Harry, then Potter. Much later, Harry appears again and it has to recall Potter. Mamba was trained on sequences about 250 tokens long. Then it was tested on sequences of over a million, 4,000 times longer than anything it had seen. It still got them perfectly right. According to the paper, none of the other models they tested got past twice their training length. Then came real language. A Mamba model with about three billion parameters matched transformers twice its size. And it generated text about five times faster than a transformer of similar size because it never has to reread everything it has already seen. It worked beyond language too. On DNA, it could tell apart humans, chimpanzees, gorillas, orangutans, and bonobos, species that share about 99% of their DNA. And on speech, a small Mamba model beat much larger systems built for audio.
So remember that secret weakness from the start. Transformers pay for remembering everything. Mamba pays only for what matters. The digits matter. The ums don't. Now let's be honest because this isn't magic. All of this was shown on fairly small models up to about 3 billion parameters. Today's biggest AI systems are far larger. And whether Mamba keeps its edge at that scale is still an open question. When a task needs one exact detail copied from far back, attention is still hard to beat. And transformers come with years of tools and experience built around them. So Mamba didn't end the transformer era, but it did prove something important. You don't have to choose between a model that remembers everything and is slow and a model that is fast but forgets carelessly. There's a third option. A model that is fast because it knows what to forget. And the idea is spreading. Many new AI systems now mix the two. Attention for precise recall. Mamba style layers for speed and long memory. For a long time, the race in AI was about remembering more. Mamba suggests intelligence might also mean knowing what isn't worth remembering at all. So here's my question for you.
